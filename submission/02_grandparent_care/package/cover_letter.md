Dear Editor,

I submit "Grandmothers' care and mothers' time: co-resident grandmothers in India" for consideration as an original article in *Ageing & Society*.

Grandmothers' care of grandchildren is usually studied through its consequences for mothers' employment, and the cost to the grandmothers themselves is rarely measured outside high-income countries. India's Time Use Survey 2024 records a 24-hour diary for every household member aged six and above, so the paper measures the same day for both generations. It compares 49,992 married mothers aged 18 to 49 with a child under six (36 per cent of whom live with a grandmother) and the women and men aged 45 to 80 in the generation above them. I also show how grandmothers and young children can be identified correctly in household rosters that do not record parent identifiers, which matters in the patrilocal households where most three-generation arrangements occur.

The main findings concern both generations. Grandmothers living with a young grandchild report much more childcare and less domestic work, free time and sleep than other older women, so family care is a substantial transfer of time with a cost to the older women who provide it. The pooled association between a co-resident grandmother and mothers' own childcare or paid work is small and is not robust across specifications; it differs by the grandmother's age and employment and by household arrangement, and I say plainly that these data do not establish that grandmothers relieve mothers. I think this speaks to the journal's readers because it treats older women as providers with their own time and wellbeing, in a setting where population ageing is under way and formal childcare is limited.
