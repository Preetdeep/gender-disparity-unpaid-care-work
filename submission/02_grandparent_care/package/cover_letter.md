Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Grandmothers' care and mothers' time: coresident grandmothers in India", for publication as an original paper in the Journal of Population Economics.

Studies of grandparental childcare mostly infer substitution from mothers' employment, and the cost of care to the grandmothers themselves is rarely measured outside high-income countries. India's Time Use Survey 2024 records a 24-hour diary for every household member aged six and above, so the paper observes the same day for both generations. It compares 49,992 married mothers aged 18 to 49 with a child under six (36% of whom live with a grandmother) and separately studies 26,062 women and 19,382 men aged 45 to 80 in the generation above a married woman of childbearing age. A further contribution is a set of relationship-based rules for assigning grandmothers and young children in rosters that record no parent identifiers, which matters in the patrilocal households where most three-generation arrangements occur.

Grandmothers living with a young grandchild report 79.5 minutes more childcare a day and less free time and sleep than other older women, so family care is associated with a substantial transfer of time with a cost to those who give it; their lower domestic work may partly reflect tasks shifted to mothers. The pooled association of a coresident grandmother with mothers' own childcare (-2.1 minutes a day, 95% CI -4.6 to 0.4) or paid work (-0.4 minutes, CI -3.8 to 3.0) is small and not robust across specifications, mothers' employment rate is 1.4 percentage points lower, and it differs by the grandmother's age and employment and by household arrangement. The paper states plainly that the estimates are conditional associations and that the data do not establish that grandmothers relieve mothers. I believe it fits the journal's interest in the family, aging and the allocation of time across generations; a recent paper in the journal (Bansak, Dziadula and Zavodny 2026) finds that living in an intergenerational household in the United States is positively related to employment among Asian women and negatively among white women; the present paper adds both generations' time in a patrilocal setting.

Transparency about related work. The manuscript is original and is not under consideration elsewhere. A preprint of an earlier version, titled "The Grandparent Care Economy: Conditional Effects on Women's Childcare Burden in India", was posted on SSRN on 26 April 2026 (abstract 6563021, https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6563021) under SSRN's standard licence (all rights reserved; no Creative Commons licence). The submitted manuscript differs substantially: it updates the method, sharpens the perspective and rewrites the text. The preprint studied women aged 18 to 50 in households with children (123,463 women), used coresidence with any grandparent and reported significance tests for mothers' childcare minutes, pooled and split by mothers' employment and by rural and urban residence. The present manuscript restricts the sample to married mothers of children under six, assigns grandmothers and children with the relationship-based rules described above, adds the time use of the older generation itself, reports confidence intervals with robustness and placebo checks, and reaches a more cautious conclusion about the pooled association.
