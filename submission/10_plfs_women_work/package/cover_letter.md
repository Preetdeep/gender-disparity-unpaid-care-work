To the Editors, Population Studies

Dear Editors,

I submit "Counting more women's work: unpaid helpers, subsidiary activity and the rise in women's employment in India, 2017–18 to 2023–24" for consideration as an original article in Population Studies. The manuscript is submitted exclusively to the journal and is not under consideration elsewhere.

India's Periodic Labour Force Survey reports that the share of women aged 15 and over who work rose from 22.0 per cent in 2017–18 to 40.3 per cent in 2023–24. The paper uses the unit-level data for both years. It reproduces the published estimates and then accounts for the rise by status in employment, by principal versus subsidiary activity, by industry, in hours of work and by the destination of output.

Own-account work and unpaid help in household enterprises account for 86 per cent of the 18.4-point rise, agriculture for 73 per cent, and women who remain mainly in domestic duties but report a subsidiary economic activity for 48 per cent. Changes in population composition do not explain the rise. Weekly hours of work per woman rose by 40 per cent, about half the rise in the worker-population ratio. In 2023–24, 42 per cent of self-employed women in primary production produced only or mainly for the household's own consumption; excluding such work, as the 2013 international definition of employment does, would put the women's worker-population ratio at 31.5 to 37.0 per cent. The paper also documents, from the published field instructions, additional prompts for probing these activities. It does not attribute the rise to any single cause or policy.

I believe the paper suits Population Studies' interest in the measurement of population and labour force characteristics and in the interpretation of large demographic and social changes in developing countries. It provides the accounting that the current debate on the meaning of the rise needs, and it shows how measures already in the survey can make the series easier to interpret and compare.
