To the Editor, Population Research and Policy Review

Dear Editor,

I submit "Counting more women's work: unpaid helpers, subsidiary activity, and the rise in women's employment in India, 2017–18 to 2023–24" for consideration as an original research article in Population Research and Policy Review. The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere.

India's Periodic Labour Force Survey reports that the worker-population ratio of women aged 15 and over rose from 22.0 percent in 2017–18 to 40.3 percent in 2023–24. Using the unit-level data for both years, the paper reproduces the published estimates and accounts for the rise by status in employment, by principal versus subsidiary activity, by industry, in hours of work, and by the destination of output.

Consistent with earlier work (Ara and Shrivastav, 2025), own-account work and unpaid help in household enterprises account for 86 percent of the 18.4-point rise and agriculture for 73 percent. Kar and Mohanan (2025) have split the rise into principal and subsidiary components year by year and bound the effect of the revised field instructions at about 2 of the 16.6 percent growth in the rural female workforce in 2023–24. The paper's contribution concerns measurement. Women whose principal activity was outside the labor force, mostly domestic duties, and who were counted as workers only through a subsidiary economic activity account for 47.7 percent (standard error 1.1; 95 percent confidence interval 45.5 to 49.9) of the full-period rise, a share estimated here with a design-based standard error. Weekly hours of work per woman rose by 40 percent, against 84 percent for the worker-population ratio in usual status (76 percent in current weekly status). In 2023–24, 42 percent of self-employed women in primary production produced only or mainly for the household's own consumption; excluding such output, the rule closest to the 2013 international definition of employment, puts the usual-status ratio at 31.5 percent (37.0 percent if only output used solely by the household is excluded). A shift-share decomposition shows that changes in composition do not explain the rise. Read against the published annual series, the timing neither establishes nor rules out an effect of the instructions. The paper does not attribute the rise to any single cause or policy.

The paper fits the journal's interest in population data, measurement, and policy. Women's employment rates feed labor force projections, gender monitoring, and assessments of demographic change, and the paper shows how measures already collected in the survey (principal-status, hours-based, and own-use-adjusted rates) make the headline series easier to interpret, in India and in other countries adopting the 2013 standards.
