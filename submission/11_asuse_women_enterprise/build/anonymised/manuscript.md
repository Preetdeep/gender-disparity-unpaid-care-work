**Abstract.** The share of India's unincorporated non-agricultural enterprises owned by women rose sharply in the last decade, a change often read as growing women's entrepreneurship. Using the unit-level data of the NSS 73rd round (2015--16) and the Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises 2023--24, we ask where the new women-owned enterprises are. We decompose the change in the number of female-proprietor enterprises into cells defined by activity, location (inside or outside the owner's home), dependence on a contractor and rural or urban area, and split the change in the female share into a within-cell and a between-cell part. We then compare the returns of these enterprises, measured as gross value added per worker, with the earnings women could expect outside them, estimated from the Periodic Labour Force Survey 2023--24. The female-proprietor share in the harmonised coverage rose from 19.5 to 26.3 per cent, driven by changes within cells. Home-based enterprises account for 56.9 per cent of the rise in the number of women owners, but contract work does not: the share of female-proprietor enterprises working mainly for a contractor fell from 31.2 to 17.0 per cent. Returns remain low. The median female-proprietor enterprise generates Rs 3,558 of value added per worker per month, and 80.1 per cent generate less than 25 days of the casual wage. The new women-owned enterprises are more independent than the old, but most are small home-based units that pay less than wage work; ownership counts should be read alongside location and returns.

**Keywords:** women's entrepreneurship; home-based work; putting-out; informal enterprises; India

**JEL codes:** J16, L26, O17, J22

**Plain English Summary.** More of India's small non-farm businesses are now owned by women. We use two large government surveys, from 2015--16 and 2023--24, to see what kind of businesses these are. Most of the growth is in very small businesses run from home. Fewer of them now work for a contractor who supplies the work and buys the output, so in that sense they have become more independent. But most of them still earn less per worker than a woman could earn as a daily wage labourer. The number of women business owners is a weak guide to women's economic progress. Policy that counts women owners should also track where they work, who they work for, and what they earn.

# Introduction

India's official enterprise surveys show a fast rise in women's ownership of unincorporated non-agricultural enterprises. In the NSS 73rd round (2015--16), 19.5 per cent of enterprises in our harmonised coverage were owned by a woman proprietor; in the Annual Survey of Unincorporated Sector Enterprises (ASUSE) 2023--24 the figure was 26.3 per cent [@mospi_nss73; @mospi_asuse2324]. The published factsheet for 2023--24 reports a rise from 22.9 per cent in 2022--23 to 26.2 per cent in a single year [@mospi_asuse2324]. Because these surveys are the main source on India's informal enterprises, the rise is widely cited as evidence of growing women's entrepreneurship.

This paper asks a simple question: where are the new women-owned enterprises, and what do they earn? Ownership in these surveys is a statement about who runs the enterprise; it says nothing about whether the enterprise sells on its own account, is run from a separate workplace, or earns more than the owner's alternatives. A woman who stitches garments at home for a contractor who supplies the cloth and buys every piece is recorded as the proprietor of an own-account manufacturing enterprise. So is a woman who runs a shop. The two are different economic situations, and the literature on home-based and subcontracted work has long argued that the first is closer to dependent wage work than to entrepreneurship [@kesar2024; @kabeer2018].

We make three contributions. First, we bring the two surveys onto a common coverage and decompose the change in the number of female-proprietor enterprises into cells defined by broad activity, location of the enterprise (in the owner's home or outside), dependence on a contractor (work done solely or mainly on contract) and rural or urban area. A shift-share split separates growth of women's ownership within cells from reallocation of enterprises across cells. Second, we profile the growth margin, the enterprises that account for most of the rise. Third, we benchmark the returns of these enterprises against the outside option of the women who run them, the earnings of women casual workers and self-employed women in the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2023--24 [@mospi_plfs2324]. This benchmark turns a statement about ownership into a statement about welfare: an enterprise that generates less value added per worker than the casual wage is a poor route out of low-paid work, whatever its legal form.

The closest studies are @bose2023, who documents a large productivity penalty for women-owned home-based manufacturing firms in the 2015--16 data, @kesar2024, who shows that women-owned informal enterprises are far more likely to work under subcontract, and @mohanan2025, who link the recent growth of unorganised manufacturing to women's job work. @shuvam2023 classify proprietorship by necessity and opportunity motives across earlier rounds. None of these studies follows the change from 2015--16 to the post-pandemic ASUSE rounds across manufacturing, trade and services, decomposes it by home location and contract dependence together, or compares enterprise returns with women's outside option. The paper also speaks to work on gender gaps in microenterprise returns [@demel2009; @fafchamps2014], on credit and ownership in India [@chaudhuri2020], on the local determinants of female entrepreneurship [@ghani2013] and on the stagnation and measurement of women's work in India [@klasen2015; @deshpande2021].

The answer to the question in the title is mixed. The rise in women's ownership is not a rise in putting-out: of the 6.7 point increase in the female share, 9.2 points occurred within cells, while the shrinking weight of home-based contract manufacturing, where women's ownership was already high, pulled the share down by 2.5 points. The number of female-proprietor enterprises working mainly for a contractor fell from 38.6 lakh to 32.5 lakh. But the growth is concentrated in home-based enterprises selling on their own account, which account for 65.3 per cent of the rise, and these generate far less value added per worker than men's enterprises or than casual wage work.

# Data

## Enterprise surveys

The NSS 73rd round (July 2015 to June 2016, Schedule 2.34) and ASUSE 2023--24 (October 2023 to September 2024) are nationally representative surveys of unincorporated non-agricultural enterprises, excluding construction, run by the National Statistics Office with a two-stage stratified design [@mospi_nss73; @mospi_asuse2324]. ASUSE succeeded the earlier rounds with a near-identical schedule, so the items we use are asked in nearly the same form in both (we map the codes item by item; see the code repository): type of ownership (with separate codes for male, female and transgender proprietors), location of the enterprise (within household premises or outside), whether the enterprise is an own-account enterprise (no regular hired worker) or an establishment, whether it works on contract and, if so, whether solely or mainly for a contractor or mainly for its own customers, whether the contractor supplies raw material or equipment, months of operation, usual hours a day, and the number of working owners, hired workers and unpaid helpers by sex. The released NSS 73rd round file holds 290,113 enterprise records and the ASUSE 2023--24 file 523,775 establishment records (the latter including residential renting); after harmonising coverage we keep 289,843 and 495,451 records.

We use the final multipliers supplied with each data set, the average of the two semi-round multipliers for the 2015--16 round as prescribed in its estimation procedure, and the stratum and first-stage unit identifiers for the design-based standard errors. To compare like with like we restrict both rounds to manufacturing (NIC 2008 divisions 10--33), trade (45--47) and other services (49--96 except 84), and drop residential renting (NIC 68108), which ASUSE tabulates separately. "Female-proprietor enterprise" means proprietary ownership by a woman; partnerships, self-help groups, cooperatives and other forms are kept as a third category.

## Returns and the outside option

Gross value added (GVA) is the survey's own item, computed from receipts and operating expenses for the reference period and annualised following the published procedure (by twelve for perennial enterprises and by the reported months of operation for seasonal and casual ones). We work with monthly GVA per worker, where workers include working owners and unpaid helpers, and restrict the returns analysis to market enterprises. GVA per worker is an upper bound on the owner's earning per worker, because it is before rent, interest and the cost of capital.

The outside option comes from the PLFS 2023--24 unit data. We take the average daily earnings of women in casual wage work in current weekly status (Rs 291 in rural areas and Rs 359 in urban areas; Rs 298 overall, against Rs 450 for men), and the average gross earnings over the last 30 days of self-employed women (Rs 5,497, against Rs 16,150 for men). A month of casual work at 25 days is therefore worth Rs 7,438; we report 22 and 30 days as sensitivity bounds.

## Validation

Before any analysis, each estimate that has a published counterpart is reproduced from the unit data. The PLFS benchmark reproduces the published quarterly all-India average wage of women casual workers in current weekly status within Rs 1.8 a day in rural areas and within Rs 7.5 in urban areas in every quarter (Rs 2.1 for India as a whole), the male all-India wage within Rs 0.7, and the published 30-day earnings of self-employed women within 0.6 per cent (Table 5 in the Appendix). For the enterprise surveys we reproduce the number of enterprises, the female-proprietor share, GVA per worker and GVA per enterprise for each round (Table 6 in the Appendix): 13 published statistics in all, including the number of workers and total GVA. The largest gap is 0.45 per cent, and every gap is within the rounding of the published figure. Two definitional points matter. First, the published female-owned share for 2023--24 is reproduced only when female proprietary establishments are divided by all establishments (26.17 per cent); as a share of proprietary establishments alone the figure is 27.64 per cent (19.54 and 20.36 per cent in 2015--16). We use the published definition throughout. Second, GVA in both rounds is reproduced only for market enterprises, that is excluding non-profit institutions and head offices (NIC 70100), with the reference-period value annualised as described above.

# Method

Let $c$ index cells formed by activity (manufacturing, trade, other services), location (home, outside), contract dependence (solely or mainly on contract, otherwise) and area (rural, urban), $N_{ct}$ the weighted number of enterprises in cell $c$ in year $t$, $F_{ct}$ the number with a female proprietor, $e_{ct} = N_{ct}/\sum_c N_{ct}$ the cell share and $s_{ct} = F_{ct}/N_{ct}$ the female share.

The *count decomposition* reports $\Delta F_c / \sum_c \Delta F_c$, the share of the rise in women owners that each cell accounts for. The *shift-share decomposition* of the change in the overall female share $S_t = \sum_c e_{ct}s_{ct}$ is
$$\Delta S = \sum_c \bar e_c\,\Delta s_c + \sum_c \bar s_c\,\Delta e_c ,$$
with bars denoting the average of the two years; the first term is the within-cell change and the second the change due to growth of cells where women's ownership is high. If the rise were driven by women entering independent enterprise across the board, the within term would dominate in outside, own-market cells; if it were driven by the growth of home-based contract work, the between term and the home and contract cells would dominate.

For returns, we report the weighted median and quartiles of monthly GVA per worker by ownership type, home location and contract dependence, the share of enterprises whose GVA per worker falls below 22, 25 and 30 days of the woman casual wage, and GVA per worker-hour. We then estimate, with survey weights and design-based standard errors, regressions of log GVA per worker on a female-proprietor indicator, urban location, NIC two-digit and state fixed effects (model 1), adding home location, contract dependence, own-account status, log hours a day and log months a year (model 2), and interactions of the female indicator with home and contract (model 3). The change in the female coefficient from model 1 to model 2 measures how much of the raw gap is accounted for by where and how women's enterprises operate, in the spirit of @oaxaca1973. These regressions describe; they do not identify the effect of ownership on returns.

# Discussion

The results reject the simplest pessimistic reading of the rise in women's ownership. Had the rise come from the spread of putting-out, with women recorded as proprietors of home-based units that stitch, roll or assemble for a contractor, the contract cells and the between-cell term would carry it. They do not. Contract cells account for -8.9 per cent of the change in the number of women owners, and the between-cell term is negative, because home-based contract manufacturing, the cell where women's ownership was highest in 2015--16, shrank as a share of all enterprises. The fall in contract dependence among women owners is large. It suggests that the subcontracting linkages documented for the earlier rounds [@kesar2024] have become less common among women's enterprises, at least in the forms the surveys record.

The results do not support the optimistic reading either. Most of the new women-owned enterprises are home-based units selling on their own account: the rural home-based manufacturing cell alone accounts for 35.8 per cent of the rise, and home-based own-market cells together for 65.3 per cent, against 43.6 per cent for own-market enterprises outside the home. These are the units with the lowest returns in the data. Their median value added per worker is below what a woman casual worker earns in 25 days, and below the average 30-day earnings that self-employed women report in the PLFS (Rs 5,497). The gap with men persists after controlling for activity, state, location, contract status and time worked, as in earlier work on women-owned firms in India and elsewhere [@bose2023; @demel2009; @fafchamps2014].

Two readings fit these facts. One is that the enterprise surveys now capture more of the low-intensity, home-based production that women combine with domestic work, which the shorter hours of women's enterprises suggest; in that case the rise in ownership is partly a rise in measured, rather than actual, activity. The other is that more women have taken up independent self-employment because wage work close to home is scarce, so that the enterprise is a fallback rather than an opportunity, in the sense of @shuvam2023. The data cannot separate these, nor can they tell whether some enterprises are recorded in a woman's name while being run jointly within the household. Both readings imply that the count of women owners is a weak indicator of women's economic progress.

Three caveats apply. First, eight years separate the rounds, and the period includes the 2016 demonetisation, the introduction of the goods and services tax in 2017 and the pandemic; the comparison describes the net change and does not attribute it to any of them. The published one-year rise in the female-proprietor share between ASUSE 2022--23 and 2023--24 (22.9 to 26.2 per cent) also suggests that part of the change is recent. Second, GVA per worker is not the owner's income: it includes the return to capital and is shared with unpaid helpers. Third, the PLFS casual wage is the earning of women who found casual work, and may overstate what the average woman proprietor could earn outside her enterprise.

# Conclusion

Between 2015--16 and 2023--24 the share of India's unincorporated non-agricultural enterprises with a woman proprietor rose from 19.5 to 26.3 per cent in a common coverage. The rise was not driven by home-based contract work, which declined, but by home-based enterprises selling on their own account, most of which generate less value added per worker than casual wage work. For research, this means that ownership is a poor proxy for entrepreneurship in these surveys unless it is combined with location, contract status and returns, all of which the surveys already collect. For policy, programmes that set targets in terms of the number of women-owned enterprises would do better to track the share of those enterprises that operate outside the home, sell on their own account and earn more than the local wage.
