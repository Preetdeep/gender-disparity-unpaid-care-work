# Editorial Review: "Beyond Physical Labor: The Gendered Mental Load of Shopping and Household Management"

## Journal of Development Economics - Style Review
**Date:** November 2, 2025
**Reviewer:** Editorial Board

---

## SUMMARY RECOMMENDATION
**MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED** - This paper tackles an important and timely topic (the "mental load") with large-scale data. The conceptual framing is strong, the writing is clear, and the descriptive patterns are compelling. However, the paper suffers from **critical data errors** (survey year, sample size claims), **measurement validity concerns** (do shopping/management time really capture "mental load"?), and **missing empirical verification** (are these activity codes actually present in the data?). The paper makes strong conceptual claims that the data can only partially support. With revisions, this could be a strong contribution to the literature on gender and household labor.

- Most people don't shop on any given diary day

  - Intensive margin (how much time if shopping) is based on small subsample
  - Averages are driven by many zeros
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Move this limitation to FRONT of measurement section
  - Add supplementary analysis: Among those who shop, how much time?
  - Discuss: Does low participation affect interpretation?
  - Consider: Is this really measuring "mental load" or just "who happened to shop on diary day"?

## MAJOR ISSUES (Substantive Problems)

### 5. **MEASUREMENT VALIDITY: DOES SHOPPING = MENTAL LOAD?**
**CONCEPTUAL CONCERN:**
- Paper frames shopping/management time as capturing "mental load"
- But the evidence for this interpretation is thin:
  - Shopping time may just reflect task division, not cognitive burden
  - A man who shops for 30 min may bear equal mental load as woman who shops 60 min
  - Time spent ≠ cognitive burden
  - Mental load includes planning, remembering, worrying—not captured by time use
- **The paper acknowledges this** (lines 210-219) but then proceeds as if time = mental load
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Either:
    1. Reframe paper as about "household planning ACTIVITIES" not "mental load" per se
    2. Provide stronger evidence that these activities proxy for cognitive burden
    3. Add more caveats throughout about measurement limitations
  - Consider alternative title: "The Gendered Division of Shopping and Household Planning"
  - Tone down claims about capturing "mental load" directly

### 6. **CAN'T DISTINGUISH PLANNER FROM EXECUTOR - SERIOUS LIMITATION**
**ACKNOWLEDGED BUT UNDERPLAYED:**
- Line 217: "If a man goes shopping but his wife made the list and decisions, we would incorrectly attribute planning to him"
- This is mentioned once then never discussed again
- But this is a FUNDAMENTAL limitation that could reverse your conclusions
- If men shop based on women's lists/plans, women bear mental load even when men shop
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Bring this limitation to front of paper
  - Discuss in results: Could this explain patterns?
  - Add to conclusion: Results may UNDERSTATE mental load gaps
  - Consider qualitative evidence: Who makes shopping lists in Indian households?

### 7. **"HOUSEHOLD MANAGEMENT" (CODE 35) UNDEFINED**
**CRITICAL AMBIGUITY:**
- Paper uses Activity Code 35 "Household management"
- But NEVER explains what this includes
- Line 207: "paying bills, financial management, coordinating repairs and services, administrative tasks"
- Is this based on data dictionary? Or assumption?
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Show what Code 35 actually captures in Indian TUS
  - If it has sub-codes, show breakdown
  - Discuss: Does this really capture management? Or just bill payment?
  - If data dictionary exists, cite it and include relevant excerpt

### 8. **BASELINE GENDER GAP (NEVER MARRIED) UNDER-DISCUSSED**
**INTERESTING FINDING BURIED:**
- Table 2 shows never-married women shop more than never-married men
- This baseline gap suggests gender norms operate even before marriage
- But paper focuses entirely on marriage gap, neglecting pre-marriage patterns
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Add subsection: "Gender Gaps Before Marriage"
  - Discuss: Why do single women shop more?
    - Living with parents—helping mothers?
    - Shopping for family even when unmarried?
    - Gender socialization from childhood?
  - This enriches the story: Marriage amplifies existing inequality

### 9. **EMPLOYMENT RESULTS UNDER-INTERPRETED**
**MISSED OPPORTUNITY:**
- Key finding: Employed women reduce shopping only 29% despite employment constraints
- Paper calls this "double burden" but doesn't fully unpack implications
- Why don't employed women reduce more? Several possibilities:
  - Can't outsource (no grocery delivery services)
  - Retain responsibility even if employed
  - Shopping is already quite low (~10 min/day)
- Also: Employed men don't increase shopping—why not?
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Add paragraph discussing mechanisms
  - Relate to Indian context: Limited outsourcing options in 2019
  - Discuss bargaining power: Do employed women have more leverage?
  - Compare to findings from other countries

### 10. **CROSS-SECTIONAL DESIGN LIMITS CAUSAL CLAIMS**
**SELECTION CONCERNS:**
- Paper mostly uses correlational language (good!)
- But occasionally slips into causal framing
- E.g., "marriage shifts planning and procurement responsibilities onto wives" (line 136)
- Selection issue: Women who shop more may marry different types of men
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Audit entire paper for causal language
  - Add explicit selection discussion in limitations
  - Consider: What would you do with panel data?
  - Frame as "associations" not "effects"

  - Carlson et al. (2020) on cognitive labor and employment
  - Offer & Schneider (2011) on class and intensive parenting
  - Munsch et al. (2020) on household management in egalitarian couples
  - Recent work on mental load during COVID-19
- Also light on India-specific literature
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Add 8-10 more recent citations (2018-2024)
  - Include India studies on household labor and employment
  - Discuss: How does Indian context differ from Western studies?

### 12. **MECHANISMS SECTION TOO SPECULATIVE**
**SECTION 6.1: "WHAT EXPLAINS THE GENDERED MENTAL LOAD?"**
- Lists several mechanisms (cultural norms, specialization, double burden)
- All plausible but no empirical traction
- Reads like literature review, not analysis
- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Either cut to 1-2 paragraphs, OR
  - Add suggestive evidence from your data:
    - Does gap correlate with domestic work time?
    - Larger in states with more traditional norms?
    - Related to household income?
  - Use heterogeneity results to test mechanisms

### 13. **POLICY SECTION GENERIC**
**SECTION 6.3: POLICY IMPLICATIONS**
- Recommendations are standard talking points
- "Make mental load visible", "technology", "paternity leave"
- Not grounded in your specific findings

### 14. **ROBUSTNESS CHECKS LIMITED**
**ONLY SHOWS HETEROGENEITY, NO SENSITIVITY:**

- But doesn't test robustness to:

- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Add 2-3 robustness checks

  - Check regional heterogeneity

### 15. **COMPARISON TO WESTERN LITERATURE WEAK**

- **FIX REQUIRED:**
  - Add paragraph comparing magnitudes
  - Discuss: Is India's gap larger/smaller than expected?
  - What explains cross-country differences?

## ADDITIONAL SUGGESTIONS FOR STRENGTHENING

### 1. Alternative Framing: "Household Planning Activities"
Consider reframing paper as measuring "household planning and procurement activities" rather than "mental load":
- More defensible given your data
- Still novel and important
- Avoids over-claiming about cognitive burden
- Allows you to say: "These activities likely reflect mental load, but we can only observe time spent"

### 2. Add Qualitative Evidence
To strengthen "mental load" interpretation:
- Cite Indian ethnographic studies on who makes household decisions
- Reference Time Use Survey qualitative components if available
- Discuss: In Indian context, who typically makes shopping lists/decisions?

### 3. Explore Who Plans vs. Who Executes
If data allows:
- Can you identify couples (same household)?
- Among couples where husband shops, does wife also shop?
- If wife doesn't shop when husband shops → maybe he's taking over
- If wife still shops → she's likely planning even when he shops

### 4. Connect to Employment Constraints
Strengthen employment analysis:
- Among employed women, does shopping occur on work days or off days?
- Does shopping time correlate with work hours?
- This would provide evidence of "double burden"

### 5. Regional Variation
India has massive regional variation in gender norms:
- Show results by state or region
- Are gaps smaller in Kerala (more egalitarian)?
- Larger in UP/Bihar (more patriarchal)?
- This tests cultural norms hypothesis

### 6. Household Composition
- Does mental load gap differ in joint vs nuclear families?
- With mother-in-law present, do both women shop?
- This connects to Indian family structure literature

### 7. Future Research Section
Add paragraph on what you'd do with better data:
- Panel data to track marriage transitions
- Couple-level data to see within-household division
- Time diary supplements asking "who decided you would do this?"
- Longitudinal tracking of mental load over life course

## FINAL NOTES

This paper tackles an important topic (mental load) that has received mostly qualitative attention. Bringing large-scale quantitative evidence is valuable. However, the paper makes strong claims about measuring a cognitive construct (mental load) when the data only capture behavioral proxies (shopping time).

**The core tension:** Mental load is about planning, remembering, coordinating, worrying—"in your head" work. Time use surveys capture activities but miss this cognitive dimension. Shopping time may correlate with mental load, but the connection is assumed, not demonstrated.

**Two paths forward:**
1. **Modest claims:** Frame as study of "shopping and household management activities" that "likely reflect" mental load
2. **Stronger evidence:** Provide better justification (qualitative evidence, theoretical arguments, empirical tests) that these activities proxy for cognitive burden

I recommend path 1 (modest claims). This makes the paper more defensible while still making an important contribution.

**Bottom line:** With major revisions, this can be a strong paper published in a good field journal (Demography, JDE, EDCC). The novelty, large data, and India focus are real strengths. But authors must address measurement validity concerns and fix critical data errors.
